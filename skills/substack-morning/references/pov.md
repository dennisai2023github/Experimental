## SECTION 1: CORE PHILOSOPHY

### What a governance program actually is

A governance program exists to enable organizations to use AI responsibly to achieve their core mandate. It is not primarily a risk system, a compliance engine, or a cultural initiative. Those are components. The purpose is responsible enablement — making it possible for the organization to do what it is trying to do, with AI, without causing harm in the process.

### Why governance programs fail

Four things kill governance programs, and they are almost never technical:

1. Missing executive sponsorship — not nominal support, but real power committed to the program
2. Poor sequencing — building the right things in the wrong order, so later work has no foundation
3. Treating governance as a compliance exercise rather than a business function — producing documents instead of decisions
4. Organizational unreadiness — deploying governance into a culture that has not been prepared to receive it

### Governance and AI deployment

The Foundation layer must exist before anything else moves. Once it is in place, other layers can build alongside early AI deployment. Governance does not wait for perfect conditions. It establishes the minimum viable structure, then keeps pace with the organization's AI ambitions.

### On frameworks

NIST AI RMF, ISO 42001, the EU AI Act, and other frameworks are starting points. They give structure, vocabulary, and regulatory grounding. They do not give a program. Every element of every framework must be heavily adapted to the organizational reality — industry, risk profile, culture, constraints. The practitioner who follows a framework without judgment is not governing. They are checking boxes.

### On speed versus thoroughness

Progress over perfection. A functioning program at 70% is worth more than a theoretically complete program that exists only in documentation. Build. Produce real artifacts. Keep moving. The ideal conditions for starting never arrive.

### On regulation

Regulation is the floor. A governance program built only to satisfy regulatory requirements is the minimum viable program, not a good one. Organizations that treat regulation as a ceiling will always be reactive. Those that treat it as a floor will be positioned ahead of the next wave of requirements before it arrives.

### On governance and innovation

Governance sets real boundaries and those boundaries matter. In practice, governance must concentrate its attention on the highest risks. A program that tries to govern everything equally governs nothing effectively. Set the boundaries clearly. Enforce them hardest where the consequences of failure are most severe.

### On maturity models

Maturity models are the right tool for assessment. They give a clear and communicable picture of where the organization stands and where it needs to go. Score on evidence, not aspiration. "We are planning to do this" is Level 0. Used honestly, maturity models are one of the most useful instruments in the program builder's toolkit.

### On organization size

All 18 governance components apply to every organization. What varies is implementation depth. A 50-person company and a 50,000-person company both need governance structure, data governance, risk management, and incident response. What those look like in practice is entirely different. Right-size the effort, not the scope.

## SECTION 2: THE ROLE OF THE GOVERNANCE PROFESSIONAL

The governance professional is four things at once, and the tension between them is part of the job:

- **Risk manager** — identifying and mitigating harm before it reaches the organization
- **Translator** — making technical realities legible to business leaders and business priorities legible to technical teams
- **Change agent** — shifting how the organization thinks about and uses AI over time
- **Business partner** — enabling responsible AI deployment, not obstructing it

The governance professional who builds things with the business will always outperform the one who builds things for the business.

## SECTION 3: EXECUTIVE INVOLVEMENT

Executives must be deeply involved. Governance without executive participation is performative. It produces the appearance of accountability without the substance.

The difference between a sponsor who attends quarterly reviews and one who makes calls when the program hits resistance is the difference between a program that survives and one that dies quietly after the first reorganization.

Business sponsorship is necessary but not sufficient. A sponsor who signs the mandate is not the same as a sponsor who provides air cover under pressure. Know the difference. Build the second kind of relationship, not just the first.

## SECTION 4: STAKEHOLDER MANAGEMENT AND ORGANIZATIONAL POLITICS

### The three groups

Every organization has three types of people in relation to AI governance:

- **Believers** — they understand why governance matters and will help build it
- **Skeptics** — unconvinced, need evidence before committing
- **Resistors** — see governance as bureaucratic overhead and push back at every turn

### The influencer-first principle

With limited time and resources, focus on the influencers first — regardless of which group they sit in. An influencer who becomes a believer converts others. An influencer who remains a skeptic legitimises the resistance of everyone around them. An influencer who is an active resistor can derail the program regardless of how many believers have been activated elsewhere.

Find influencers on Day 1 of stakeholder mapping. Engage them before you need them. Build the relationship before the first difficult conversation.

### On politics

Organizational politics is real and must be acknowledged. The governance program does not operate in a frictionless organization. Politics is addressed where it creates specific program risks — sponsorship gaps, stakeholder resistance, competing priorities — and readers are given tools to navigate it. The governance professional's job is to manage those relationships tactfully, persistently, and without losing credibility. The goal is not to win arguments. It is to manage relationships.

### The continuous two-way conversation

AI governance is not a document exercise. It is a continuous two-way conversation with leadership and business teams that never fully settles. The governance professional must maintain that conversation consistently — not just at quarterly reviews, not just when something goes wrong. Ongoing dialogue is the mechanism by which governance stays connected to organizational reality and retains the trust it needs to function.

## SECTION 5: PROGRAM OWNERSHIP

The program is built with the business from day one. It should never feel like it belongs to the governance function alone. Components are designed with named business owners, embedded in operational processes, and handed off with accountability structures that survive leadership changes.

Co-creation produces ownership. Ownership produces adoption. A framework that governance built alone and presented to the business is a framework the business will work around. A framework that business units helped design is one they will defend.

## SECTION 6: DATA GOVERNANCE

Data governance is both a prerequisite and a parallel workstream, and consistently the hardest part of AI governance. Most organizations discover mid-build that their AI governance is only as strong as their data governance — and their data governance is not as strong as they thought.

The governance response to unmapped, unowned, or poor-quality data is to continue mapping and classifying as-is and flag quality issues as findings. Fixing data quality is an operational responsibility, not a governance one. Governance maps, classifies, flags, and escalates. The solving belongs to the business.

---

## SECTION 7: VENDOR RISK

Vendor failures are your failures. That is the baseline position.

In practice, governance attention must concentrate on the highest-risk vendor relationships — foundational model providers, systems touching consequential decisions, partners with access to the most sensitive data. Assess everything. Monitor the ones that matter most.

When existing vendor contracts are missing mandatory governance clauses, the response is not to trigger immediate renegotiation across the board. Document all gaps, communicate them to the governance body, and build a renegotiation roadmap that is risk-ranked and time-bound.

## SECTION 8: EXPLAINABILITY

If you cannot explain a model's decision, it should not be deployed. This is not a technical argument — it is a governance argument. Decisions that cannot be explained cannot be audited, defended, or improved.

Explainability requirements are set by the consequence of the decision, not by technical convenience. The more consequential the output, the higher the explainability standard. Technical limitation is not a governance exemption. If a model cannot meet the explainability standard for its use case, the options are to replace it, constrain its use to decisions that are human-reviewed before action, or accept the governance and regulatory risk with full documentation and governance body sign-off.

## SECTION 9: AI LITERACY

AI literacy is a governance prerequisite, not a training program. Without it, governance decisions are made in the dark by people who do not understand what they are governing.

AI literacy cannot begin until the Acceptable Use Policy exists. You cannot educate employees on acceptable use until acceptable use has been defined. The moment the AUP is complete, the literacy program starts — and it continues forever. Every policy change triggers a literacy update. Every new AI tool triggers role-specific training. Every incident review identifies literacy gaps that feed back into the program.

The requirement is role-differentiated:
- **Executives**: strategic literacy — what AI can and cannot do, what governance requires of them
- **Operators and business users**: working knowledge — what is permitted, what requires approval, what is prohibited
- **Technical teams**: practitioner depth — model lifecycle, governance gates, documentation standards

AI literacy programs are consistently underfunded and undervalued relative to their governance impact.

## SECTION 10: AI INCIDENTS

AI incidents are inevitable regardless of how well-built the program is. The question is never whether an incident will occur. It is whether the organization can contain it, learn from it, and come out with a stronger program than it had before.

The first-hour protocol when an AI system is causing active harm: **contain first, classify second, communicate third.** Suspend or constrain the model immediately. An uncontained system making the situation worse makes every subsequent decision harder. Stop the bleeding. Then understand what happened. Then notify the right people in the right order.

This is why the incident response plan must be built before it is needed. The first hour of a real incident is not the time to design the containment procedure.

## SECTION 11: AUDIT AND CONTINUOUS MONITORING

Audit and continuous monitoring are complementary. Neither replaces the other.

Continuous monitoring is where the real governance value lives. It catches problems in real time. In Year 1, invest in making monitoring genuinely work rather than building a formal audit programme. The formal audit follows in Year 2 when systems are more mature, the monitoring baseline is established, and auditors have something meaningful to audit against.

Most organizations treat audit as a compliance checkbox. It is the proof layer — it validates that the program is working as a system, not just component by component.

## SECTION 12: PROGRAM MATURITY AND THE 90-DAY FRAME

### What Day 90 actually means

Day 90 is the architecture completion point, not the program completion point. Most organizations will not complete every component in 90 working days. What they have at Day 90 is the right things started in the right order, with the right owners, and a clear view of what comes next. Anyone who says the program is finished at Day 90 is selling something else.

### On uneven maturity

Uneven maturity across components is normal and expected. Different components mature at different rates depending on organizational complexity, resource availability, and the maturity of underlying functions. Not every component needs to reach the same maturity level. A highly regulated financial services firm needs its regulatory compliance and risk management components at Level 4. The same firm may function well with AI literacy at Level 2 for several years.

### The re-assessment is a checkpoint, not a verdict

The Day 90 re-assessment is not a success or failure review. It is a strategic checkpoint that produces a realistic picture of where the program stands and feeds directly into roadmap and investment decisions for the next 18 to 24 months. The governance body leaves that session not with a grade but with a direction.

### On the Year 1 calendar

Build a flexible Year 1 operations calendar with quarterly recalibration points rather than a rigid schedule. A calendar that cannot flex becomes irrelevant the moment organizational priorities shift. Quarterly recalibration keeps the program connected to current organizational reality without abandoning structure entirely.

## SECTION 13: CONTINUOUS COMPONENTS

Several governance components run past Day 90 and do not stop. They require permanent operational owners, defined review cadences, and escalation mechanisms:

- **C4 — Regulatory Monitoring**: regulation does not stop evolving
- **C7 — Change Management**: every new AI deployment requires a change response
- **C11 — AI Literacy**: runs as long as employees use AI tools
- **C12 — Risk Management**: updated as the AI portfolio evolves
- **C14 — Vendor Management**: monitoring never stops
- **C16 — Performance Monitoring**: runs as long as AI systems are in production
- **C18 — Audit and Compliance**: continuous monitoring plus periodic formal audit

The most common failure mode after Day 90 is drift — components that functioned during the build phase slowly stop functioning because no one is actively maintaining them.

## SECTION 14: WHAT THIS PLAYBOOK DOES NOT COVER

This playbook sets up a governance program. It does not cover technical implementation. Technical papers exist for that. The objective is the program itself — the structure, the ownership, the processes, the accountability.

The real enforcement of governance controls happens at the execution level — when those controls are integrated into workflows and reduce friction between policy requirements, technical controls, security obligations, and the day-to-day work of people. That is a different and deeper layer of governance work. There is significant discussion in the industry about governance happening at decision time, at execution time. That is the right direction and the industry needs to move there. This playbook does not go there. It builds the frame. A future version may address that deeper layer.

## SECTION 15: CLOSING ASPIRATION

*In Dennis's own words:*

I see a world where governance is a service — where the controls we spend 90 days designing are not checkpoints that interrupt work but capabilities embedded so deeply into how decisions are made and actions are taken that they are simply how things get done. Where a developer building an AI system has governance built into the tools they use. Where a business analyst requesting a model deployment has the approval pathway built into the workflow. Where an employee using an AI tool for the first time has the relevant policy surfaced at the moment of use, not communicated in a training session six months earlier.

That world is not far away. What is missing in most organizations is the program foundation that makes embedding those controls meaningful rather than performative. You cannot integrate governance into execution if you have not first defined what governance requires. You cannot enforce controls at decision time if you have not first established who has the authority to set them.

That is what this playbook builds. Not the destination. The foundation that makes the destination reachable.

## SECTION 16: DAY-BY-DAY DECISION POSITIONS

The following captures Dennis's specific practitioner positions on 20 scenario-based questions from the 90-Day Playbook. These positions are woven into the playbook narrative as default instruction. They are captured here as standing reference for how Dennis thinks through real implementation decisions.

---

**Day 1 — No executive sponsor found**
Stop everything. Do not proceed to Day 2. Document the gap formally and escalate before any further work begins. A programme without a named sponsor who has real budget authority has no foundation. Supportive conversations are not sponsorship.

**Day 2 — Self-assessment bias in baseline scoring**
Triangulate every score with at least two sources. Interview the person responsible and at least one person working inside the function. Where scores conflict, treat the lower score as the working hypothesis until evidence says otherwise.

**Day 3 — Conflicting lenses on target state**
Balance all three lenses — business criticality, risk exposure, and regulatory obligation — and where they conflict, let the governance body make the call with documented rationale. That is precisely what the governance body exists to do.

**Day 4 — Pressure to include disengaged senior leaders on the governance body**
Prioritise genuine commitment over seniority. A governance body populated by disengaged senior leaders is a liability. A committed mid-level manager who attends every meeting and makes real decisions is worth more than a disengaged executive who lends their name to the charter.

**Day 5 — Designing escalation pathways**
The escalation design follows the authority structure defined in the charter. Each role owns decisions within their domain. Disagreements escalate to the AI Council. The Chief AI Officer — or the equivalent role carrying ultimate AI accountability — is the final decision-maker. The pathway is the charter made operational.

**Days 7–8 — Pushback on AI principles from technical teams**
Present all six principles together as a coherent system. Principles introduced separately get adopted selectively, which undermines the framework. When technical teams push back on individual principles, the response is to show how removing that principle breaks the coherence of the entire system.

**Day 8 — AUP balance between strict and permissive**
Three-part approach: start with where the organisation actually is, tier controls by risk level, and publish immediately. A short clear imperfect policy communicated today is worth more than a comprehensive policy still in legal review next month. Then tighten incrementally.

**Day 9 — Opening AI literacy with three different audiences**
Lead with context before rules. Explain why the policy exists and what harms it is designed to prevent. Then ground it in the tools people are already using. Context first, reality second, rules third.

**Days 10–11 — Building one compliance view across overlapping frameworks**
Map all applicable frameworks simultaneously and build one unified compliance view. Where frameworks overlap, build the control once and map it to all applicable frameworks. Where they conflict, document the conflict, escalate to the governance body, and resolve with written rationale.

**Days 12–13 — Foundation checkpoint with incomplete component**
Run the checkpoint regardless of completion status. Document exactly what is complete, what is in progress, and what is missing. Use the findings to decide whether Phase 2 can start on any stream. The decision belongs to the governance body, not to the governance professional.

**Day 21 — Insufficient capacity to run three parallel streams**
Assess actual capacity honestly, sequence the streams accordingly, and communicate the adjusted timeline to the governance body before Day 21. Capacity constraints are not a governance failure. Hiding them is.

**Days 21–23 — Discovery of unauthorized AI tool in production during infrastructure assessment**
Treat it as a governance finding. Document it in the risk register and follow the escalation pathway defined in the governance charter. The pathway exists precisely for this moment. Using it on the first serious finding validates the entire governance architecture.

**Days 24–28 — Unmapped, unowned data assets**
Continue mapping and classifying data as-is and flag quality issues as findings in the register. Fixing data quality is an operational responsibility, not a governance one. Assign the remediation to the right operational owner and keep moving.

**Days 29–33 — Business pressure to deploy a model that has not passed the approval gate**
Escalate to the governance body. A deployment decision made under business pressure without meeting the approval gate is a governance body decision, not a governance professional decision. The first time the gate is tested under pressure is the moment that determines whether the programme is real or performative.

**Days 21–24 — Risk register reluctance from domain owners**
Build the risk register collaboratively with domain owners in the room. Reframe the register as a shield, not a sword. A documented risk that is identified and managed is a governance success. An undocumented risk that becomes an incident is a governance failure.

**Days 25–29 — AI security controls without CISO engagement**
Integrate AI-specific threat assessment into the existing cybersecurity programme and engage the CISO as a named co-owner from day one. Security controls without CISO ownership are recommendations, not controls.

**Days 30–36 — Existing vendor contracts missing mandatory clauses**
Document all gaps in the vendor register, communicate them to the governance body, and build a renegotiation roadmap that is risk-ranked and time-bound.

**Days 21–25 — Use case matrix results conflict with executive preference**
Score on business outcome in its full sense — not financial return alone. Business outcome includes risk reduction, regulatory compliance, operational resilience, and strategic positioning. Present the matrix findings to the governance body and let them absorb the political pressure.

**Days 26–30 — True programme cost significantly exceeds leadership expectations**
Present the full cost model to the CFO with no softening. Simultaneously identify what can be absorbed by existing business unit budgets rather than requiring central funding. Honesty without a solution is just bad news.

**Days 31–42 — Believers, skeptics, and resistors in stakeholder map**
Focus on the influencers first, regardless of which group they sit in. Believers need activation, skeptics need evidence, resistors need executive pressure from the governance body — not from the governance professional working alone.

**Days 56–60 — Black-box model in high-consequence use case**
Explainability requirements are set by the consequence of the decision. A credit decisioning model sits at the highest end of the consequence scale. If the model cannot produce a human-readable explanation, the options are to replace it, constrain its use to human-reviewed decisions, or accept the risk with full documentation and governance body sign-off. Technical limitation is not a governance exemption.

**Days 61–66 — Drift threshold conflict between business units and central governance**
Start with anomaly-based monitoring that flags performance relative to baseline. Sidestep the political fight over fixed thresholds in the early days. As the programme matures and production data accumulates, migrate toward consistent governance-set standards.

**Days 67–71 — Active AI system causing customer harm**
Contain first, classify second, communicate third. Suspend or constrain the model immediately. Then understand what happened. Then notify the right people in the right order per the escalation pathway.

**Days 72–75 — Formal audit versus continuous monitoring in Year 1**
Use the continuous monitoring programme as the primary validation mechanism in Year 1. Invest in making monitoring genuinely work. Build the formal audit programme for Year 2 when systems are mature enough to audit meaningfully.

**Days 76–80 — Mixed re-assessment results with leadership expecting stronger outcomes**
Uneven maturity across components is normal. Different components mature at different rates. Not every component needs to reach the same level. The re-assessment is a diagnostic tool that informs the Year 2 investment case — not a report card on programme performance.

**Days 81–85 — Programme validation session framing**
The re-assessment is a checkpoint, not a verdict. Frame the validation session as a strategic review that produces a realistic picture of programme status and feeds into roadmap and investment decisions for the next 18 to 24 months. Not a success or failure review. A direction-setting session.

**Days 86–90 — Year 1 operations calendar follow-through**
Build a flexible calendar with quarterly recalibration points rather than rigid fixed commitments. A calendar that cannot flex becomes irrelevant the moment priorities shift. Each quarter is a touchpoint, a checkpoint, and an opportunity to re-anchor the programme through the ongoing conversation with leadership.

## SECTION 17: VOICE AND TONE REFERENCE

When generating content that reflects Dennis's point of view, apply the following:

**Tone**: Honest but constructive. Name the problem clearly, then give the path forward. The reader is a professional who has encountered these problems before. They do not need to be protected from the truth. They need the truth delivered in a way that produces action rather than paralysis.

**What to avoid**:
- Em dashes
- Self-answered rhetorical questions
- Tricolon lists used as emphasis
- Punchy fragment runs
- Filler transitions ("it is worth noting that", "importantly")
- Patronizing analogies
- AI-sounding polish

**What to aim for**:
- Writing that sounds like a knowledgeable person thinking on the page
- Naturally varied sentence length — some short, some longer
- Some messiness allowed — not everything needs a punchline
- Peer-to-peer register — practitioner to practitioner, no lecturing
- Direct statements of position — not hedged, not softened unnecessarily

**Political acknowledgment**: Politics is real and acknowledged where it creates specific program risks. The playbook does not pretend organizations are frictionless. But it stays focused on the governance work rather than becoming political commentary.
